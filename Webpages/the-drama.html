The Drama - Reviewing the Guy’s Dilemma

It’s a romantic film with two very, very attractive people — of course I am sold.

The Drama — Plot

Emma (Zendaya) and Charlie (Robert) are truly in love with each other and preparing for their impending wedding. They are writing their wedding speeches, doing dance rehearsals, finalising the menu — all while looking spot-on and being cutely in love. This modern love loses its normality when Charlie, along with two close friends at a “finalising-the-menu dinner”, discovers that Emma had planned a mass shooting as a teenager. The remaining film explores how Charlie and Emma’s “relationship of love” responds to this twisted fact.
Before anyone else can react, the maid of honour (Rachel) makes her shock and contempt at this obviously disturbing piece of information very clear. She is enraged and disgusted by Emma because her cousin has been paralysed from another unrelated mass shooting that had actually happened. Her exaggerated reaction symbolises one side of society for me.
This also scares the shit out of Charlie for different, more natural reasons. He feels like he no longer knows who he is in love with — the sexy, funny, empathetic Emma or a funny-looking, unfunny teenage psychopath who plans mass shootings. Rachel’s husband (Mike) tries to bring Rachel back to her senses while Emma, moments after her confession, pukes on the dinner table. I would have called her reaction unreasonable had I not known someone who had puked his entire childhood when anxious. Emma was clearly anxious at being lashed out at. She was also scared and sad.

Then begins our travel into Charlie’s mind, as he starts thinking and overthinking about Emma. A teenager who would plan a mass shooting would be violent, temperamental, and awkward — he thinks. He fills in these assumptions by recalibrating certain incidents from their relationship so that she can be better understood by him.
Suddenly — her anger at a driver for not slowing down at the crosswalk, her embarrassment at an older picture of herself, her slapping him in the middle of sex — all add up to form this image of a violent and disturbed woman capable of executing a mass shooting.
While all this happens on one hand, he also allows Emma to explain herself. Now, we as an audience can understand her easily. But he can’t, because Emma’s explanation and his imagination are not synced. Both of them visualise her story very differently because they are humans with finite imaginations and infinite assumptions. This makes them miss out on details we, as viewers, can see.

It’s especially highlighted through a scene where a teenage Emma tries to record a threatening video of herself with a rifle but is unable to do so because of technical fucking glitches. We, the audience, can view it objectively and see that she is just an innocent kid. But Charlie doesn’t.
Emma is embarrassed about going over these silly details. Charlie cannot imagine how an empathetic 30-year-old can also be a violent 12-year-old. He is unable to understand her, hence accept her — forget being able to love her. Which is why, all through the film, Emma is being manhandled on the sidelines.
Enter the second half of the film, and Charlie, while still unconvinced, starts defending Emma in his mind mainly because he can’t cancel the wedding — that’d be a social blunder. So he starts trying to love her.

He already loves whatever he knows of Emma. He doesn’t love her for her sociopathic initiative. So, he changes his approach to acceptance. Instead of thinking from his brain, he starts thinking from his dick. His licentious imaginations of an inviting Emma in lingerie, holding a rifle, glue him back to this relationship. When the parasite of misunderstanding and mistrust eats through their relationship, Charlie tries to save it via lust. It clearly doesn’t work. He has fallen out of love, and the charade falls apart too. And that’s the drama!

The Drama — Beyond the Plot

One can also wonder — would this have happened differently if the conversation had taken place only between the two of them and not with fucking Rachel around? If they didn’t have the pressure of putting up with a performative wedding which demands speeches, dances, and social approval? If society didn’t have norms for who deserves love? Charlie has low EQ, which is a Charlie-problem. But he also has prejudices, which is again a Charlie-problem, and on a deeper level, a society problem.

Assumptions of society can become prejudices. And prejudices make our societies unyielding and tone-deaf. She committed to planning such a pathetic thing. She was recording threatening videos. She was practicing with her gun. She deserves xxyyzz. That’s our efficient decision-making system.Societies believe all truth to exist in action, not in thoughts and feelings. No one cares for what lies underneath. Love need not believe in this. Maybe that’s why Emma could accept Charlie even after HE CHEATED ON HER.

The film is an insight into how people think and love. It may seem dramatic because the characters are emotional and thoughtful people. However, that’s a joke on us — we who are too busy doing all things important but not thinking and feeling for people. I really enjoyed my time with Charlie and Emma, trying to figure it out. Once in the film, Charlie says if love is radical acceptance… I think it’s a promise laced with so much trust that radical acceptance becomes a natural consequence.
